    <div class="case-study-body">
      <p>In 2018, I joined a small hyper-casual game startup. We built dozens of prototypes, testing different game concepts and moving quickly from one idea to the next based on early product metrics.</p>

      <p>Eventually, one concept performed well enough to keep developing: a car falling down a mountain, smashing through the environment and gradually destroying itself along the way.</p>

      <p>I handled the programming and built the first playable version in roughly one to two weeks, with most of my attention going into game feel: tuning the falling, impacts, destruction, and sense of constant progression. Despite its rough form — simple Unity Terrain, cube-based environments, and a basic upgrade economy — the first test already showed strong product metrics.</p>

      <p>Over the next year, that prototype evolved into a full game. The core gameplay became deeper and more varied, while the project gained a much richer progression structure, vehicle customization, economy, and other meta features that gave players more reasons to keep playing beyond the original mechanic.</p>

      <p>My work covered the technical side of the project end to end. I led development together with one junior programmer, working alongside a lead designer and the founder driving the product, while owning the overall technical direction as the game grew.</p>

      <p>Later, we developed a larger-scale sequel, pushing the same idea toward more realistic visuals and considerably more complex physics, including deformable geometry and scenes with hundreds of destructible objects.</p>

      <p>A major part of my work was physics-heavy: vehicle behavior, destructible environments, and preserving the feel of movement and destruction as the game became more complex. Beyond the core gameplay, I worked across UI, vehicle customization, progression and monetization features, subscriptions, third-party SDK integrations, and the broader architecture needed to support the game as it expanded.</p>

      <p>The most valuable part of this experience was the breadth and continuity of ownership. The technical approach I established in the first prototype became the basis for the game’s feel and strongly shaped how it evolved afterward. Building dozens of prototypes also gave me a strong practical sense for rapid gameplay iteration, while taking one of them all the way into a long-running commercial game gave me much deeper exposure to mobile marketing and to the engineering decisions required once an experiment becomes a real product.</p>
